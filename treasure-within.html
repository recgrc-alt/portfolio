The Treasure Within

2026 · Interactive Web & 3D · Developer: Programming, UI/UX & 3D

With Daniel Costa (3D Assets)

An immersive 3D web experience inspired by Disney's Treasure Planet. You pilot a voxel galleon through space toward planets that embody human emotions: Happiness, Courage, Love and Hope. The real treasure is internal, not gold.

Developed in the 3rd year, for the Multimedia Communication course, with Daniel Costa, the project reinterprets the Disney film around personal growth. Daniel produced the base 3D assets while I led the development: Three.js programming, sensor integration, performance optimisation, rigging and sound engineering.

To hold a fluid 60 FPS in the browser I followed a strict technical pipeline. Models started as voxels in MagicaVoxel, passed through Blender for geometry cleanup and polygon reduction, and characters were rigged in Mixamo before everything was exported as .GLB. A global LoadingManager pre-loads every 3D and audio file into memory before the experience starts. I replaced heavy opacity maths with native Additive Blending for the planets' holographic glow, and simulated the universe with a skydome whose dynamic colour filters shift the lighting according to the visitor's real local hour.

The core of the code dropped menus and read the real world instead. I wired the Web Audio API to the microphone so the player must raise their voice on the Planet of Courage, used MediaPipe to read the camera and confirm a smile on the Planet of Happiness, built a rhythm mini-game with anti-cheat cooldowns on the Planet of Love, and a glitch simulation on Hope that tests the visitor's patience. The journey closes on a PHP/JSON backend where travellers leave reflections for others. An exhausting project, and one awarded top marks.

Concept and vision

Reinterpreting Sci-Fi Nostalgia

The starting point was Disney's Treasure Planet (2002), a film where a nineteenth-century sailing galleon crosses space looking for Captain Flint's treasure. Watching it again, we realised the gold is the least interesting part of it.

The film is about Jim Hawkins growing up, finding his confidence, and finding a father figure where he was not expecting one. That reading is the one we took all the way: here the treasure is internal.

We built the Etherium, a sea of stars with four planets, and every planet is an emotion: Courage, Happiness, Love and Hope. The visitor does not watch a Captain on screen, the visitor is the Captain.

The sky follows your computer's local clock, so whoever arrives at three in the morning does not see the same Etherium as someone arriving at midday.

The galleon you pilot, from every angle.

Voxel and Cyber-Sailpunk

We modelled the whole world in blocks, in MagicaVoxel. It reads well at any distance: a planet two hundred units away still has a silhouette, and a cube costs the GPU almost nothing.

On top of that we put a green-phosphor CRT terminal. We called it cyber-sailpunk: nineteenth-century wood, rigging and sails living beside computer interfaces and thrusters. It is the film's own logic, moved over to the software side.

The same block was the rule for everything. Ship, crew and planets came off the same grid, and the planets had to be taken apart into separate meshes in Blender so the rings and satellites could turn on their own.

The crew alternates between idle and walking, and each one carries a hitbox so it cannot walk through the hull. Morph is the exception: no skeleton at all, animated in code with sines and cosines, which for a blob of jelly worked better than any rig.

No Ball Leaves in a Straight Line

Ballistics, trails and thrusters

Each cannonball starts with an initial velocity and is then under a constant downward acceleration, which traces an arc. Since there is no ground in the Etherium, the ball never hits anything: it keeps falling and drifts away until it is gone.

We kept the endless fall because it sells the scale of the void better than any explosion would. Attached to every ball is a trail that dissipates behind it, and that is what keeps the trajectory readable once the ball is far away.

The thrusters follow the same logic: the count and size of the particles scale with the ship's speed, so when you accelerate you see the engine pulling. The sound follows, rising and falling with acceleration, and you hear your speed before you look at the screen.

A Terminal That Notices You

There are no menus anywhere. The opening terminal shrinks when the game starts and goes to live in a corner of the screen. It is the one that asks your name and uses it from then on: everything that in another game would be a pause panel is here the terminal talking to you.

The tutorial lives in the same place and is implicit. The game blocks until you press W and fire the thrusters, and only then asks you to fire the cannons. There is no instructions screen: you learn to move by moving.

It is also the terminal that writes the Captain's Log while you play. By the end of a session there is a document that did not exist before, different from anybody else's, and you take it with you. It is the only part of the Etherium that still exists after you close the tab.

Since visitors write into the Log and leave messages on the Mural dos Viajantes, the travellers' wall, that text is treated as untrusted: it goes through an escapeHTML() before it reaches the DOM, with a banned word filter on top. It is dull work, but the moment someone can write onto another person's screen it stops being optional.

Four Emotions, Four Input Channels

Every planet is an emotion and every emotion has a mechanic of its own. The ambition was to use every input channel the browser gives us, the microphone, the camera, the timing between keypresses and the complete absence of action, so the code reads the real world instead of counting clicks. A click is always the same click. A shout is not.

In Courage, the microphone feeds a Web Audio API analyser reading volume in real time, and you have to hold three seconds above the threshold. Courage is rarely about swords, it is about having the strength to break the silence, and if you play in silence you do not get past this.

In Happiness we open the camera and run MediaPipe's FaceLandmarker, looking for a smile. In Love you hear a heartbeat and have to press in time with it: if you mash the key, your progress goes backwards, because love needs time and needs you to align with someone else's rhythm.

The last one is Hope, and it is doing nothing. The terminal starts glitching and looks like it has crashed, and the only way through is to press nothing at all and wait for it to stabilise on its own. It works against instinct, because when a screen fails the normal reaction is to hit every key.

The Players Who Froze and the Planet That Crashed

In the user tests, people entered the game and simply stood still looking at the ship. We had built a world with total freedom of flight and nobody realised they could fly. That is where the decision came from: let the terminal teach, instead of having instructions.

Then came the opposite problem. With total freedom, players flew off into the void and got lost, with no reference to come back to. The terminal learned to notice you had left the route and to offer to recalculate, with a sonar sound pointing the way. It stays an offer, not a forced correction.

On the model side, the MagicaVoxel exports carried thousands of hidden vertices nobody could see in the viewer. The first planet we put into the code brought the game down, and every model had to be cleaned by hand in Blender, one at a time.

Rigging in Mixamo was another nightmare: rubber skeletons, Silver's arm bending the wrong way, Jim simply refusing the rig. And we asked AIs for help with the collision and camera maths, which broke the code further and left the ship flying upside down. The hard part we had to work out on paper.

This Runs on a Phone

Portrait and landscape, and the same four mechanics

This runs on a phone, and it is not a cut-down version. It was a challenge we set ourselves at the start: the same experience, whole, in portrait and in landscape, so the project had to solve two formats instead of one.

The four mechanics did not change. The shout, the smile, the heartbeat and the wait work in exactly the same way, because a phone's microphone and camera are the same sensors the browser was already using.

What changed is what you cannot see. The particles were reduced, and the sky, which turns constantly on a desktop, only turns on a phone while the ship is stopped. There is no sense spending processing on movement nobody notices while they are sailing.

The controls are the one thing designed from scratch. A round pad to steer the ship, and a firing button that appears when there is something to fire at.

Who Did What

I, Rogério Correia, was the lead developer. I built the Three.js structure, the cameras, the lights, the game loop, the MediaPipe integration, the Web Audio API, the rhythm logic behind the Love mechanic and the project documentation. Daniel Costa modelled 3D assets in MagicaVoxel. From there the models were mine to finish: the cleanup in Blender, the rigging and the animation through Mixamo, the scenery built around them, and most of the concept the game rests on.

The split was not neutral and it was not drawn from a hat. We chose it because one member of the team was less comfortable with programming, and giving him a defined piece of the modelling was how both of us could produce usable work inside the deadline. Saying it plainly is more honest than pretending it was an even division of tasks.
