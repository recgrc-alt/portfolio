Abrigo

2026 · UI/UX & Product · Product Designer · Researcher · Frontend Dev

With Afonso Almeida · Pedro Teixeira · Tiago Gomes

An emergency app for civilians during a war, made for a phone with almost no signal, almost no battery and somebody frightened holding it. Shelters, a kit that keeps itself up to date, and a way to call for help in one movement.

Design for the worst day

One disaster, chosen and stuck to

The brief was to design an interactive artefact that helps people before, during or after a disaster, and to pick one kind and stay with it. We chose war.

It brings its own rules with it, and they are the opposite of the ones an app is usually built under. No internet worth relying on. Possibly no power. Infrastructure that may already be broken. And a person using it who is frightened, in a hurry, and not going to read anything carefully.

So the target was never a beautiful app. It was one that still works on the worst day of somebody's life.

Five people, five different emergencies

The same app, needed for opposite reasons

Before drawing a single screen we wrote who this was for, and it was not one person. A young man walking home who needs to know which streets to avoid. A mother on shift at a hospital whose first thought is the school her children are in. A civil first responder who gets an alert that somebody near him needs help. A preparer who keeps an emergency kit and wants it checked. An elderly man, alone, who suddenly cannot breathe.

Each of them got a scenario written as a story, from the ordinary moment before, through the alert, to how it ends. Those stories are what set the app's parts: a live map with the areas to avoid and safer routes, shelters, an inventory that warns you when the food and medicine are close to expiring, a way to reach people, and a call for help that goes out with your location and your medical details attached.

The last one is the one that decided the design. If Alberto can only manage a single movement, then everything else on this app has to be arranged around that movement being possible.

Broken on paper first

Wizard of Oz, before anything was real

Nothing was made in high fidelity until the idea had been through people. We tested on paper, with a Wizard of Oz set-up: the person holds the sketch and acts, and one of us plays the part of the app, changing the screen by hand as they go.

It is a cheap way to be told you are wrong. The button to call for help was pressed by mistake over and over, by people who thought it meant general help with using the app. Others went to the emergency kit looking for their own profile. The tips page was read as tips on how to use the app rather than as what to do in an emergency.

All three got fixed there and then. Asking for help became a slider instead of a button, so it cannot happen by accident, and it stopped being called Ask For Help and became SOS. Being told this on paper cost an afternoon. Being told it after the interface was built would have cost the interface.

We built the thing that tested it

Not a form, an instrument

For the real usability testing we did not want a questionnaire someone fills in afterwards from memory, so we built our own site to run the sessions.

It takes the participant through the whole protocol. An intake questionnaire and a consent step first. Then the tasks, one at a time, each of them timed by the site itself, with the person rating how hard it was as soon as they finish it. Then a closing questionnaire about the language, the colours, the type and whether they could see themselves using it under stress. Everything lands in one place, and there is an administrator area that reads it all back as charts.

It is the difference between remembering that a task felt slow and knowing how long it took. That is the site below, still running.

Black, and one yellow

The colour of a warning sign

The interface is black with a single yellow on it. Black because a screen that is mostly dark asks the least of a battery and of a pair of eyes at night, and the yellow for the contrast against it and because it is the colour this kind of information already has in the world, on the signs that mark caution and danger.

Everything else is about a hand that is not steady. Large targets, high contrast, the fewest steps a critical action can be done in, and type on the Material Design 3 scale, which we then audited the interface against rather than only borrowing from.

The full high-fidelity design lives in Figma, and the prototype below is the one we presented.

And then it was built

Not a click-through, a set of pages

The prototype did not stop in Figma. The core of the app was written as real pages, so the screens that matter can be opened on a phone and used with a thumb instead of clicked through in a viewer.

It is the home, the map, the emergency kit, the messages and the tips page, sharing one stylesheet with the palette and the type scale in it. Opening it on an actual phone is the only way to find out whether the targets really are big enough, and no viewer will tell you that.

Top marks, and a name they liked

The process was the point, and it showed

The project got the top mark. The teacher liked the name, and at the end of the presentation we were congratulated for the work.

What was being marked was never only the app. The course was about going through every step of an interaction design process properly, and this is the project where we argued the most, went back the most times, and threw away the most. That is the reason the last version is the one it is.
