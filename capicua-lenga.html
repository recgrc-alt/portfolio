Capicua, Lenga

2025 · Motion & Audiovisual · Illustrator & Frame-by-frame Animator

With Carolina Seixas

An animated music video for Lenga, by the Portuguese artist Capicua. Every frame drawn one at a time, in the register of a child's drawing, with a girl walking through the places the lyrics name.

Taking the lyrics at their word

A school, a history book, a mountain

The exercise was a music video and we had to pick a song from a list. We settled on Capicua first, as the artist, and then went looking through her for the right one. Lenga won because of what it is about: childhood, school, the things you are taught. Carolina loved the lyrics, I came round to them, and that was that.

We pulled lines out of the song and built the places they name. A school. A history book. A mountain range. Nothing is a metaphor for the line, it is the line, drawn.

It is literal and symbolic at the same time, and that is the trick of it: a viewer follows the images without needing to decode anything, and the meaning arrives through the song rather than through the picture explaining itself.

A girl who says nothing

The thread between the places

A set of illustrated places is a slideshow. What makes it a film is a person moving through them, so a main character was added: a child who crosses each space and connects them into one small journey.

She never speaks and nothing on screen explains her. Everything she is comes from the body: how she stands, how she moves, how fast she goes. It is the oldest way to carry a narrative and it is the one that does not compete with a song already full of words.

She is wearing headphones in almost every scene, which is the small thing that ties the whole video together. She is listening to the song we are listening to, and the places she walks through are what it is putting in her head.

Drawn the way a child draws

Simple line, soft shapes, lettering by hand

The visual register comes from children's drawings. A simple, unfussy outline, soft organic shapes, and lettering done by hand rather than typed. The words on screen are few, the title and little else: the song is doing the talking and putting its lyrics up as well would have been saying everything twice.

That is not a shortcut, it is a match. The song is about childhood and about the things you are told when you are small, and a slick, corrected, perfectly aligned video would have argued with it. The imperfection is the argument.

Every frame drawn once

No copying, no interpolation

The animation is frame by frame, and the rule we set was that no drawing gets reused. Every frame is its own drawing, made from scratch, across every scene in the film.

It is a brutal way to work and it buys one specific thing. Because no two frames were drawn identically, the line never sits still: it shifts and wobbles very slightly from frame to frame, which is the texture people recognise from hand-drawn animation and which no software will fake convincingly.

It also enforces a discipline. When every frame costs the same amount of work, you stop animating things that do not need to move, and the film gets tighter as a result.

A minute and a half that keeps moving

The drawing never quite settles

The finished video runs a minute and twenty-eight seconds, and there is not a still moment in it, even where nothing is happening. The line itself is always breathing.

For a minute and a half of film it is a great deal of drawing, and the reason for doing it that way is visible in every second of it.

Made two-handed

One song, split down the middle

It went well, and again the mark is not the interesting part. This was a good project to be inside: one song, one rule, and a colleague to argue with about what a verse looks like.

The problems it threw up were the enjoyable kind. How many drawings a walk needs before it stops reading as a stutter. How to keep a face recognisable when you have redrawn it from nothing over and over. Where to let the line go loose, because the song is loose in that bar too.

Carolina and I worked on all of it together rather than dividing it into halves, which for something drawn frame by frame is the only way it gets finished at all.
